01

What Sweep is, and how it works under the hood

Sweep (sweep.io — not the unrelated sweep.dev AI coding tool) markets itself as "the agentic workspace for Salesforce." For a keep/sunset decision, the label matters far less than one architectural fact: Sweep runs two distinct execution surfaces, and they carry opposite rebuild costs and opposite conflict risk. Everything downstream in this dossier depends on which one Fortra is running.

Both surfaces install into the customer org as a managed package — Sweep is an in-org, AppExchange-listed app, and it states plainly that customer transactional data never leaves the org: "Sweep operates natively inside Salesforce. It executes logic within your environment and does not export or store your transactional customer records externally" (operational-excellence, faq). What differs between the two surfaces is where the automation logic actually runs.

Surface A · Async managed-package engine
Classic "Sweep Automations" & the Ops runtime

The original RevOps product: routing, dedup, alerts, and the trigger/condition/action automation builder. This logic runs on Sweep's own async engine inside its managed package, not as native Flow.

  • Runs "on the record type level," "asynchronously when appropriate" src
  • "Splits DML statements into separate transactions"; schedules "100+ daily jobs on one Salesforce job slot" src
  • Governor-limit bypass is only possible off the native synchronous path — the tell that this is a parallel runtime
  • Opaque & un-orderable relative to RLM pricing triggers
Surface B · Native metadata generation
"Build Mode" & the funnel/gate config

The newer builder generates genuinely native Salesforce Flows that appear in Flow Builder, and the funnel/gate/field/validation config deploys as real metadata.

  • Produces an Active before-save record-triggered Flow visible in Flow Builder src
  • Atomic deploy of field + Flow + FLS + page layout via Metadata API; "detects existing flow conflicts" src
  • Funnel constructs "the necessary backend infrastructure in Salesforce" as native config src
  • Inspectable, versioned, orderable — rebuildable like any Flow

A third, newer layer sits on top of both: an agentic / Metadata-Agent capability that reads and documents the org — dependency graphs across objects, fields, Flows, Apex, triggers, validation rules, CPQ, and managed packages; plain-English documentation that stays current; and change-impact simulation "before deploying" (salesforce, metadata-agents). This layer is read/advisory — it recommends what to merge/retire and proposes Flow patterns, but the current site frames it as evidence-with-impact-context rather than autonomous deployment. It therefore adds no runtime conflict risk of its own. High

The one distinction that drives everything

If Fortra's footprint is Surface B (Build Mode / native Flows), sunsetting is comparatively cheap and safe: the output is real, versioned, inspectable Flow metadata you can diff against RLM triggers, reorder, guard, and re-own. If it is Surface A (the async engine), the logic lives inside Sweep's opaque runtime — you cannot sequence it against pricing, so a sunset means extracting that logic into governed native automation. Determining which surface is live is the single most decision-relevant finding, and it requires org access.

Corrections applied from verification
  • The often-quoted line "Sweep operates on its own optimized engine rather than generating native Salesforce Flows" was not found on the live page. The parallel-runtime conclusion stands by inference from the governor-bypass behaviors (and third-party corroboration that Sweep automations "can't be ordered alongside Flow"), but it is not a verbatim Sweep self-description — do not present it as one.
  • The phrase "components introduced through managed packages" refers to Sweep analyzing the customer's existing managed packages — not Sweep describing its own runtime. That Sweep's own runtime is a managed package is a sound inference from the AppExchange listing + in-org execution, but its exact namespace and component footprint remain undisclosed.
02

Capability inventory

What Sweep does today, grouped by capability. Grades reflect how well each is evidenced, not how good it is.

Automations High

Trigger / condition / action builder for RevOps workflows across Leads, Opportunities, Cases, and custom objects. On Surface A this runs async on Sweep's engine with per-step logging and error isolation ("if one transaction fails, it won't disrupt other processes"). Sweep's own FAQ is explicit that it does not replace Flow and positions itself as complementary — its edge is UX and governor-limit ergonomics for low-admin teams, not raw capability flow-vs-sweep.

Routing & assignment High

A multi-object routing engine — "the same logic applies whether you're assigning leads to SDRs or accounts to enterprise reps." Matching runs at record creation (real-time, not batch), with round-robin and weighted distribution, PTO/capacity-aware balancing, SLA timers and escalation, and dedup embedded "at the moment of assignment." All logic "runs natively inside Salesforce with audit trails and deployment controls" and is configured with no user-authored Apex/Flow salesforce-routing.

Data hygiene & deduplication High — Sweep's strongest genuine differentiator

Real-time dedup and matching "right as records are created or updated," with fuzzy or exact matching across multiple fields, cross-object linking (Lead↔Account/Contact), auto-merge across leads/contacts/accounts/cases, enrichment during matching, and "immediate updates across related records (tasks, opportunities, events)" duplicate-management. This addresses concrete native ceilings (below) that routinely drive orgs to third-party tools.

Why native dedup is not enough
  • Max 5 active duplicate rules per object; no cross-object detection (the classic Lead-is-also-a-Contact case)
  • Native merge is manual only, same-object only, and capped at 3 records at a time — no auto-merge
  • A matching rule that flags more than ~2% of records (the default Identical Match Key Threshold; hard cap 5%) is blocked from activating
  • Fuzzy matching has no field weighting (email can't be made to outweigh first name)

Caveat, from verification: the fine-grained merge-config claims (customizable tie-breakers, merge-vs-enrich-vs-override control, field-preservation) were not present on the live pages and read as marketing paraphrase — treat cross-object real-time auto-merge as confirmed, the granular knobs as unverified.

Alerts & notifications High

Slack / Microsoft Teams / email alerts built "without Apex, Flows, or dev support," off any object or field update, embedding dynamic record data (opp value, owner, next step). Crucially bi-directional: reps can update stages, close dates, and fields directly from Slack/Teams — explicitly positioned beyond native connectors' "limited, one-way notifications." "Playbook Alerts" can be delegated to GTM leaders without exposing core config alerts.

Funnel / GTM visualization High — differentiator

A design-time canvas that maps the whole org — Leads, Opportunities, Cases, custom objects — onto a single cross-object view, with funnel steps and "gates" governing what data must be filled and whether steps advance automatically or by user action. Native Flow Builder shows one flow at a time and Setup one object at a time; there is no native single-canvas cross-object funnel. Independent reviewers cite this as one of Sweep's two most-defensible advantages ("makes config legible to non-admins") clientell.

Territory-based assignment High

Territory-aware routing by geography, account tier, industry, or any Salesforce field — a configuration feature of the routing engine, executed declaratively, complementary to rather than a replacement for native Enterprise Territory Management (ETM). ETM models the access/hierarchy; it does not do round-robin or set lead ownership. Sweep fills the assignment/ownership half lead-routing.

Org documentation & dependency graph High — differentiator

Deterministic dependency graphs across objects, fields, Flows, Apex, triggers, validation rules, CPQ, and managed packages; change-impact simulation before deploy; and always-current auto-generated documentation. There is no native org-wide equivalent — native tooling ("Where is this used?", the dependency API) is partial and manual. Independent review calls the auto-documentation "genuinely good (saves the doc-drift problem)." Overlaps Elements.cloud / Sonar, not anything native salesforce.

AI / agentic layer High — read/advisory

Documentation, Monitoring ("continuously scans for risks, misconfigurations, and governor-limit threats"), and Permissions agents; process mining; natural-language Q&A ("what updates this field?"); and Agentforce-readiness assessments. Advisory, not autonomous deployment — though a "Build mode" appears on higher pricing tiers, so whether upper tiers can write/deploy is a genuine open question metadata-agents.

Dashboards / reporting Medium

Sweep does not sell a BI/dashboard runtime. Its "dashboard" story is real-time Slack/Teams alerts plus funnel-conversion visualization on the canvas; native Reports & Dashboards / CRM Analytics benefit indirectly from Sweep-cleaned data. No source describes a proprietary reporting layer that supersedes native. Note: some earlier reporting quotes could not be re-verified after a site reorganization — treat the specific wording as unverified, the negative conclusion as plausible by absence operational-excellence.

05

Revenue Cloud Advanced interaction & conflict risk

There is no published material on Sweep interacting with RLM pricing procedures, ExpressionSets, or CPQ pricing at runtime High. Sweep only reads and documents CPQ/pricing config for its dependency graph — it does not participate in price calculation. Every conflict below is therefore inferred from Sweep's confirmed runtime mechanics plus Salesforce's documented order of execution — flagged as such, not asserted as fact. The dominant risk is not "Sweep breaks the pricing math." It is that a Sweep automation writes to a Quote/Order/line field that a pricing procedure reads, on a different (often asynchronous) clock, outside the transaction where pricing ran.

  • Stale price / missed repriceMediumInferred

    Surface-A automations run async in separate transactions. A Sweep write to a pricing input (quantity, discount, term, product, currency, a custom driver) lands after the RLM pricing procedure already computed net price. Nothing re-triggers pricing — procedures are invoked by the save/reprice flow, not by a later async DML from another package. Result: a line whose driver says one thing but whose net price reflects the pre-Sweep value. Same failure class Fortra already tracks internally as stale-context / conditional-rollup-left-stale, now caused by an external writer.

  • Recursion / reprice loopMediumInferred

    If a Sweep action writes a field that is itself a price-impacting trigger or a native record-triggered-flow entry condition, the Sweep write becomes a second save that re-invokes pricing/native flows. Because Sweep runs in its own transaction, it does not share the native run's static-variable recursion guards — the usual in-transaction protections don't cover the cross-transaction Sweep write. On high-line-count RLM quotes/orders this intersects Fortra's known SOQL:101 / CPU-limit fragility at order completion.

  • Last-writer-wins field clobberingMediumInferred

    If Sweep and an RLM procedure/ExpressionSet both write the same field on Quote/Order/QLI/OrderItem, whichever transaction commits last wins — silently, with no error. Because Sweep commits later in a separate transaction, it is structurally positioned to win and overwrite the pricing-derived value. Same shape as Fortra's documented over-stamping bugs, introduced by an external writer. Mitigation: enumerate every field Sweep writes on those four objects and diff against the read/write set of every active pricing procedure step; any intersection is a defect candidate.

  • Bimodal governor interactionMediumInferred

    "Sweep avoids governor limits" is true only for Surface A and false for Surface B. (a) Surface-A async work uses the managed package's own separate 150-DML / 100-SOQL allocation — DML load invisible to native-limit reasoning that can still contend for the same rows RLM is repricing (row-lock / UNABLE_TO_LOCK_ROW risk). (b) Surface-B native before/after-save Flows run inside the same transaction as RLM pricing, stacking directly onto an already governor-heavy save — exactly the regime where Fortra already hits SOQL:101 and CPU limits. Caveat: the separate governor bucket applies to certified managed packages; confirm Sweep's package certification tier in-org.

  • RLM DML-lock silent no-opLowInferred

    Under RLM with Subscription Management enabled, certain Quote/Order line DML is blocked at the platform tier regardless of API — a constraint Fortra already tracks. If a Sweep async job attempts such a write, the failure occurs in Sweep's isolated transaction ("if one transaction fails, it won't disrupt other processes") — so it is swallowed/retried rather than surfaced to the user's save. The effect is a Sweep automation that looks configured and green but whose writes never land: silent data drift, not a visible error.

Containment posture — the crux deliverable

Because you cannot sequence an async package job inside the pricing transaction, the safe rule under Surface A is blunt: forbid Sweep from writing any pricing-input or pricing-output field on Quote, Order, QuoteLineItem, or OrderItem. Confine it to non-pricing side-effects — routing, ownership, alerts, activity. Under Surface B, containment is ordinary Flow discipline: diff against RLM triggers, place in the flow-trigger order, guard with entry conditions. The conflict set can only be finalized by inspecting which fields Fortra's Sweep config actually writes — an org task, answerable only with access.

06

Experience Cloud / Partner Portal interaction

Sweep's entire public surface is silent on Experience Cloud, partner communities, partner portals, and external/community users — a consistent absence across every routing, automation, FAQ, and AppExchange page reviewed, corroborated by targeted search High. Sweep is an internal RevOps/admin tool. Its surfaces are the internal workspace and Slack/Teams alerts; it exposes no partner-facing UI. (Note the naming trap: Sweep's "Partner Program" is its own reseller/SI channel — nothing to do with a customer's Experience Cloud partner community.)

Because Sweep's automation executes at the data/platform tier on standard objects (Lead, Account, Opportunity), it is architecturally independent of which UI or user created a record. A lead created by a partner-community user is an ordinary Lead row, so Sweep's engine would act on it like any other. This means Sweep can, in principle, route and reassign partner-created records — but this is inference, not documented Medium Inferred.

The real, unmanaged risk — sharing & visibility

Experience Cloud external users see records only through restrictive org-wide defaults plus Sharing Sets / sharing rules keyed to their Account/Contact. If Sweep routing reassigns ownership of a partner-created lead to an internal rep or a different owner/account, the partner community user can silently lose visibility of that record unless sharing is explicitly preserved. Round-robin / territory reassignment — a documented Sweep behavior — is precisely what triggers this. Sweep's materials never address sharing rules, Sharing Sets, record visibility, or execution context, so this interaction is entirely unmanaged in its documentation and must be validated by the implementer.

The decisive unknown is Sweep's execution context: does its in-org automation run in system context (without sharing) or as a specific integration/Automated Process user? That determines whether Sweep can even see partner-created records under Fortra's Partner Portal OWDs — and whether reassignment strands them. Undocumented publicly; org-level confirmation required.

Open questions requiring Fortra org access — the SC-3104 blockers

This dossier is built entirely from public sources. The keep / expand / reduce / sunset decision cannot be finalized without org access. These are the blockers, ordered by decision impact.

  1. Which Sweep surface is actually deployed — the async managed-package engine, native-Flow Build Mode, or both? Why it blocks: This single fact drives rebuild cost, conflict risk, and the entire containment strategy (§01, §03, §05). How to answer: inspect the installed package namespace, Apex classes/triggers, custom objects/CMDT; check whether Sweep-authored Flows exist in Flow Builder.
  2. What is the exact managed-package footprint — namespace, Apex triggers (and on which SObjects), custom objects, custom metadata types? Why it blocks: Determines whether Sweep triggers register on Quote / Order / QuoteLineItem / OrderItem / Product2 and where they sit in the order of execution relative to RLM pricing triggers. Undisclosed publicly; read from the installed-package detail.
  3. Which specific Quote / QuoteLineItem / Order / OrderItem fields does Fortra's Sweep config read and write? Why it blocks: The conflict set (§05) can only be finalized by diffing this list against the read/write set of every active RLM pricing procedure / ExpressionSet step. Any intersection is a defect candidate. Pure org-inspection task.
  4. Does any Sweep automation touch pricing-relevant fields or trigger repricing? Why it blocks: A Sweep write that triggers a reprice could collide with RLM's Quote DML lock and Fortra's documented reprice-before-activate sequencing. If yes, containment moves from "monitor" to "block."
  5. In what context does Sweep's automation run — system (without sharing) or a specific integration/Automated Process user? Why it blocks: Governs whether Sweep can see and reassign partner-created records under Partner Portal OWDs, and whether reassignment strands them from the partner's view (§06).
  6. Is Sweep's package certified — i.e., does it get the separate 150-DML / 100-SOQL governor allocation? Why it blocks: The magnitude of the "invisible DML load" argument (§05) depends on certification tier. The AppExchange listing implies security review but not necessarily the certification that grants the separate bucket.
  7. What is Sweep's SOC 2 / AppExchange security-review status and data-residency posture — and the true Enterprise price? Why it blocks: Procurement can't close without these. Sources say to "review" security posture but don't state the outcome; Enterprise pricing for a three-org consolidation is quote-only (§04).
  8. Does the higher-tier "Build mode" / agentic layer ever write or deploy metadata autonomously? Why it blocks: If Fortra keeps Sweep for governance only, this determines whether it stays purely read/advisory or introduces its own deployment risk (§02).
Recommendation framing for leadership

Sweep's defensible value is concentrated in three places native Salesforce genuinely does not reach: cross-object dedup/auto-merge, the org-wide dependency-graph + auto-documentation, and the cross-object funnel canvas. Its routing/alerts/automation are strong but overlap native (and LeanData/Kubaru) more than they are unique. The risk is concentrated entirely in the async-engine surface colliding with RLM pricing — a risk that is real but containable by policy (fence Sweep off pricing fields) rather than fatal. A defensible interim posture: keep for hygiene + documentation, fence off Revenue Cloud pricing objects, and defer the expand/reduce call until the three org-access blockers (Q1–Q3) are answered. This is a framing, not a final verdict — the verdict needs the org.
